Clear for Merchants

Scope: this app is for financed transactions only. A member paying from their balance or tapping a Clear card runs on ordinary payment rails and never appears here. Every figure in this reference is financed volume — do not add card or balance payments to any total, and do not build a “paid now” state.

Designed tablet-first. The counter device is a tablet in a stand or a laptop in the back office — a phone is the fallback, not the target. A separate app from the member app: different user, different job. Someone at a counter with a queue behind them, using it forty times a week. Installs as a PWA in any browser; the loaner phone is just a device with it already open.

Showing a code is the default path, not one of two options. Most customers at a counter are new, and it is the only route that never dead-ends — a new customer installs from it, an existing member just approves. Scanning their code and entering a phone number are shortcuts underneath it.
Breakpoints. Above ~900px the counter runs two columns — the action on the left, context on the right. Between 520 and 900 it collapses to one column with the action first. Below 520 it is the phone layout. Nothing is removed at any width; only the arrangement changes.

04 — The three real scenarios

Every counter interaction is one of these. The first path covers all three, which is why it is the default.

A · new customer, at the counter

Writer enters $940 and turns the screen.

Customer scans → app installs → signs up → links their bank.

The charge is waiting for them at the end of signup.

Most common at merchants one to five.

B · existing member, at the counter

Same screen, same gesture.

Customer scans → app opens → approves. No signup, no application.

Or the writer taps scan their code if the customer already has theirs open.

Becomes the majority as the corridor fills.

C · signed up in the waiting room

Writer hands over a card or pamphlet with the job estimate.

Customer signs up on their own while they wait, deciding in their own time.

Writer raises the charge; it reaches them by text, email and push.

No queue, no pressure — the best version.

Path C is worth designing for, not just tolerating. A customer deciding alone in a waiting room, with the estimate in front of them, makes a better decision than one deciding at a counter with people behind them. It is also the path where they actually read the terms.

The counter card is the artefact that makes C work — a printed code that goes home with the estimate. It is the same code the tablet shows, so nothing new has to be built.

All three end identically, at the waiting state. A writer never has to remember which path a customer took.

13 — Merchant onboarding

The merchant signs themselves up, in the app, on the tablet or their own laptop. For merchants one to five a rep is sitting there — but the flow must not require one, or merchant twenty needs a visit that does not scale.

The last step is the one that decides whether the shop transacts. Signing takes four minutes and any owner will do it. Training the counter takes fifteen and is the step people skip — so it is inside the flow, not a follow-up email.

Desktop is the primary view for onboarding — an owner does this at the back-office computer, not standing at the counter. Step rail left, one step at a time on the right. All six shown in order; the frame is identical throughout.

14 — Codes, and what they change

A code is a terms mechanism, not a referral tag. Standard terms are the default screen everyone sees. A code replaces some of them, and different codes carry different replacements — founding partners now, and later a regional launch, a category pilot, or a partner-referred shop.

Entering a code

Do you have a code?

KAI–1104

FOUNDING PARTNER · 2 OF 5 LEFT

Changes your rate and waives the fee on your first twenty charges.

No code is fine. Standard terms are what most shops sign.

Show what a code changes before it is applied, and show the scarcity honestly. “2 of 5 left” is true and it is the reason a shop signs this week instead of thinking about it. A code that silently improves terms is a discount nobody thanked you for.

Codes are per-terms, not per-rep. One code can be handed to many shops; a different code carries different terms. That keeps attribution and terms separate, so a rep is never negotiating — they are handing over a code that already means something.

Open questions

Settled: a refund closes the plan, returns the member's principal, and the co-op keeps the carry already paid — they held the money, and that is what carry is for. Still to settle in the contracts: how the ledger unwinds it, since the plan's position has to close without leaving a stub.

Partial refunds. One tyre of four goes back. Does the plan resize, or does it close and reopen at the new amount? Resizing is kinder and much harder.

Does a declined charge tell the merchant anything? Drawn above as a flat no. “Over their limit” would help a writer offer a smaller amount, and is not the merchant's business.

Merchant notifications. The customer's are designed; the merchant's are not. A confirmation at 9pm is useful; forty of them a day is noise someone turns off.
