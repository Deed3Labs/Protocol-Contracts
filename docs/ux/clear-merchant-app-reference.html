Clear for Merchants

Scope: this app is for financed transactions only. A member paying from their balance or tapping a Clear card runs on ordinary payment rails and never appears here. Every figure in this reference is financed volume — do not add card or balance payments to any total, and do not build a “paid now” state.

Designed tablet-first. The counter device is a tablet in a stand or a laptop in the back office — a phone is the fallback, not the target. A separate app from the member app: different user, different job. Someone at a counter with a queue behind them, using it forty times a week. Installs as a PWA in any browser; the loaner phone is just a device with it already open.

Showing a code is the default path, not one of two options. Most customers at a counter are new, and it is the only route that never dead-ends — a new customer installs from it, an existing member just approves. Scanning their code and entering a phone number are shortcuts underneath it.
Breakpoints. Above ~900px the counter runs two columns — the action on the left, context on the right. Between 520 and 900 it collapses to one column with the action first. Below 520 it is the phone layout. Nothing is removed at any width; only the arrangement changes.

04 — The three real scenarios

Every counter interaction is one of these. The first path covers all three, which is why it is the default.

A · new customer, at the counter

Writer enters $940 and turns the screen.

Customer scans → app installs → signs up → links their bank.

The charge is waiting for them at the end of signup.

Most common at merchants one to five.

B · existing member, at the counter

Same screen, same gesture.

Customer scans → app opens → approves. No signup, no application.

Or the writer taps scan their code if the customer already has theirs open.

Becomes the majority as the corridor fills.

C · signed up in the waiting room

Writer hands over a card or pamphlet with the job estimate.

Customer signs up on their own while they wait, deciding in their own time.

Writer raises the charge; it reaches them by text, email and push.

No queue, no pressure — the best version.

Path C is worth designing for, not just tolerating. A customer deciding alone in a waiting room, with the estimate in front of them, makes a better decision than one deciding at a counter with people behind them. It is also the path where they actually read the terms.

The counter card is the artefact that makes C work — a printed code that goes home with the estimate. It is the same code the tablet shows, so nothing new has to be built.

All three end identically, at the waiting state. A writer never has to remember which path a customer took.

07 — Payouts: two balances, not one

Owed and held are different kinds of money. What the co-op owes is a claim — scheduled, and released only as far as the payout pool allows. What sits in the cash account is already the merchant's, released and movable at any hour. Showing them as one figure would misstate both, and it is why a withdrawal has to ask where the money is coming from.

13 — Merchant onboarding

The merchant signs themselves up, in the app, on the tablet or their own laptop. For merchants one to five a rep is sitting there — but the flow must not require one, or merchant twenty needs a visit that does not scale.

The last step is the one that decides whether the shop transacts. Signing takes four minutes and any owner will do it. Training the counter takes fifteen and is the step people skip — so it is inside the flow, not a follow-up email.

Desktop is the primary view for onboarding — an owner does this at the back-office computer, not standing at the counter. Step rail left, one step at a time on the right. All six shown in order; the frame is identical throughout.

Nothing here asks a rep to touch anything. The bank step says so explicitly, because a partnerships hire standing over a laptop while an owner types account numbers is the single worst moment in the whole relationship.

20 — How this maps to Privy Organizations

Privy shipped organization wallets in August. The shape fits, and it moves three things out of the app that were going to be honour-system restrictions.

The merchant is an organization

One org per shop, with a default key quorum that owns its wallets

The owner is the key quorum

Owns and administers the wallet; provisions scoped access to others

Clear's backend key is the scoped signer

One authorization key per org, capped by policy. The device holds only a session

The approval cap is a policy

Amount thresholds enforced at the wallet layer

The refund step-up is an intent

Asynchronous authorisation collected from the owner

The refund flow was already an intent before we knew the word for it. Privy's intents system exists to collect approvals from people who are not all online at once — which is precisely “Jen requested it, Mike approves from his phone.” Section 16 does not need redesigning; it needs implementing on a primitive that already exists.

The $1,500 cap stops being a UI restriction. Privy's policy engine enforces amount thresholds at the wallet layer, so the ceiling holds even if the counter app is compromised or bypassed. That is a materially different security claim than “the button is disabled”.

Quorum approvals are available if wanted. Sensitive actions can require a threshold of named people rather than one. Not needed for a four-person shop; worth knowing exists before designing around its absence.

Recommendation: do not build against intents for v1. Enterprise pricing at five merchants is the wrong economics, and the fallback is invisible to the user — the refund request lives in Clear's records, the owner approves in the app, Clear's backend signs. No screen changes. What intents actually buy is a property Clear does not have yet anyway: signatures collected such that Clear's own server cannot act alone. That becomes worth paying for when the co-op wants to claim it cannot move merchant funds unilaterally. Migrating later is cheap, because the intent sits behind Clear's own approval record.
Recommendation: the approval cap lives in MerchantRegistry, not in a Privy policy. It is business logic — it varies per merchant, moves with volume, and forms part of the agreement — so it belongs in the protocol and should hold whichever signer or path a transaction arrives through. Use the Privy policy as a coarse dollar ceiling, a backstop against a compromised backend key, and let the contract enforce the precise rule. That also removes the need to answer whether policies support per-contract, per-method caps before starting.
Settled: a signer can be an authorization key, not only a person. Privy's owners-and-signers model accepts a user, an authorization key, or a key quorum — so Clear's backend holds one P-256 authorization key per merchant org, registered as a signer and scoped by policy. Staff need no Privy identities, and the counter device holds no signing material at all.

Which corrects the model above in one place. The enrolled device is not the signer — it holds only a session against Clear's backend. That is strictly better: a stolen tablet carries nothing, revoking a device is an instant server-side action, the policy cap applies to every device at once, and there is one key per merchant to rotate rather than one per tablet. The enrolment screen's claim — enforced by policy, not by this app — stays true and is now more precisely true.

One real gating item: intents are an Enterprise feature. The docs say so and point at sales. The refund step-up depends on them. Confirm tier and pricing before building against it; a fallback exists — hold the request in Clear's records and have the owner's approval trigger a single signed action — which is less elegant and works.

Why staff should not be Privy users regardless: a counter worker will not manage keys or recovery, turnover means provisioning and deprovisioning per hire, and the wallet belongs to the business rather than the employee. Jen raising a charge is Mike's Tire acting.

Privy also covers the fiat rails — deposits and payouts, with Bridge underneath. That bears on PayoutPool and the manual multisig boundary in the contracts plan, which is a separate conversation.

A wallet's entity cannot be changed once set, and an org is capped at 150 wallets. Neither binds a shop, but both matter if merchant wallets are ever provisioned before the org exists.

Open questions

Settled: a refund closes the plan, returns the member's principal, and the co-op keeps the carry already paid — they held the money, and that is what carry is for. Still to settle in the contracts: how the ledger unwinds it, since the plan's position has to close without leaving a stub.

Settled — partial refunds are full refunds in v1. Resizing a plan means recomputing the schedule against carry already accrued on the larger balance. Refund in full and raise a new charge for the corrected amount: inelegant, correct, and it uses machinery that already exists.

Still open — does a declined charge tell the merchant anything? Drawn above as a flat no. “Over their limit” would help a writer offer a smaller amount, and is not the merchant's business. The only one of these still genuinely undecided.

Settled — a daily digest, plus immediate alerts only for money moving or action needed. A payout landing, a refund awaiting approval. Never per charge: forty a day is a notification someone switches off, and then they miss the refund request.

Settled — shared email is fine for an owner who is also a member. Forcing two addresses gets you mike+shop@, which defeats it, and verification is scoped to the entity either way. The real fix is the account switcher: Clear shows “Kai Moore” and “Mike's Tire” as two contexts and is explicit about which you are acting as.
