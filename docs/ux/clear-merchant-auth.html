Merchant app — signing in

Three levels of authority, and only one of them is a login. The device is enrolled once by the owner. Staff start a shift with a PIN, which attributes charges rather than securing them. The owner signs in with Privy for anything that moves money.

A PIN is attribution, not authentication. Four digits on a counter tablet is not a security boundary — it will be watched and shared. What it buys is knowing who raised a charge, which is what makes the staff name on every row real. The security boundary is the device, enrolled once by the owner and removable in one tap. A stolen tablet is the risk; a guessed PIN is not.

No password field. Privy signs in by emailed code, passkey or an existing wallet. A password box would imply Clear stores a credential it does not hold, on the one screen that controls the money.

The subline says what this is for — and, more usefully, what it is not for. An owner who thinks signing in is required to take a payment will sign in on a shared tablet and leave it signed in.

Passkey is offered second but is the better path on a device the owner uses often. Email code is the fallback that always works.

Enrolment states the ceiling before it asks for consent. The owner is granting a device the ability to spend on the shop's behalf, and the sentence that matters is the one about what it cannot do.

“Remove it any time, from any device” is the sentence that makes a lost tablet survivable, and it is the reason the device rather than the PIN is the real control.

Authorisation
Staff have no wallet and no Privy account. The merchant is the organisation, the owner is its admin, and the wallet belongs to the organisation. Jen raising a charge is Mike's Tire acting, not Jen acting.

Why not give staff accounts: a counter worker will not manage keys or recovery, turnover means provisioning and deprovisioning identities per hire, and the wallet is the business's rather than the employee's.

The scoped credential is what makes it work. The owner authorises the device once; staff actions run through it, capped at the approval limit and restricted to raising charges. Nothing on the counter can initiate a payout.

Owner code

The owner code is a convenience, not a second authority. It unlocks an action the owner has already been asked to approve, on a device the owner already enrolled. It is not a way to sign in as the owner — that still needs Privy.

This is the pattern already used for refunds, and it should be the only one. Every privileged action — refund, payout, terms, staff, bank — steps up the same way.

Remote approval is the default path, because the owner is usually not standing there. The code exists for when he is.

Open questions

Does the scoped credential expire, and on what? A session that never expires is a tablet that works forever after it leaves the shop. Expiry on a schedule is safer and means an owner who is away for two weeks comes back to a dead counter.

Is the owner code the same as the owner's shift PIN? Convenient if yes, and a real weakening if the owner types their shift PIN in front of staff forty times a day. Probably two different numbers, which is one more thing to remember.

What happens to an open shift at closing time? Auto-ending at a set hour is tidy; a charge raised at 5:58pm and confirmed at 6:15pm should still attribute to whoever raised it.

Does Privy Organization Wallets cover this shape? Announced Aug 28. If org membership plus a session signer maps onto owner-plus-enrolled-device, this is mostly configuration. If it assumes every actor is a Privy user, the staff model needs to live entirely in Clear's own records.
