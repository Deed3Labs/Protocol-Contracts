Staff.

Who can use the shared tablet, what each role can do, and the one number an owner sets: how much a manager can approve.

Why this page exists

  • The tablet is shared and the roles are not. Without accounts, whoever picks it up has the owner’s powers — and the first time a writer taps Withdraw out of curiosity, the relationship is over.
  • Three roles, not a permission matrix. Counter, Manager, Owner — each a whole set rather than a grid of checkboxes. A shop with four staff wants to say what someone is, not tick nine boxes.
  • Three blocks, as on Home. Who is on the counter leads, because it is the live fact; a temporary slot appears only while someone is waiting to start; the slab holds what is always true. Guidance and prompts retire themselves here the same way setup does on Home.
  • Hours are the plan; the PIN is the record. The owner books who works when, in one modal reached from the person. Starting a shift with a PIN is what actually happened. Home’s clock reads both and never merges them, so a late start stays visible.
  • A PIN, not a login. Nobody types a password forty times a shift. Four digits on a shared device, with the owner on a real sign-in because they reach the money.

Staff · owner · tablet

The same three blocks as the member Home: the live component, a temporary slot, then the slab. The live thing is everyone on shift, as one strip of tiles. It stays one row at every width: cards share the row while they fit and scroll sideways when they do not, so a busy Saturday makes the row longer, never the page taller. Whoever has the tablet is framed in ink and tagged. The cards are the component, so nothing boxes them: a heading line, the row, and a footer line, sitting on the page. Here that is Mike, signed in to check the week, while Jen and Luis work the counter. Each tile is Home’s clock in small: since, from the PIN; until, from their hours; and one block per booked hour. Mike has no hours booked, so his tile has a start and a dashed rule where the blocks would be. Tap a tile for that person’s sheet, which is where ending someone else’s shift lives. The footer keeps the two things the person holding the tablet does about themselves. The second block is only there while it is true: somebody added who has not started a shift yet, and so has no PIN. It is the member Home’s slot exactly, one statement, one line, one action, and it goes the moment Ana starts her shift.

This week sits between who is on now and the slab: the live fact, then the plan, then what is always true. It is one day at a time at every width, because that is the version that reads. On the landscape tablet the day cards are the week at a glance, the same on every view: the day, the shop’s hours, and a cover bar along the bottom. This week shows every kind of day: Monday and Tuesday are short at the end, Wednesday and Friday are fully covered, Thursday has a gap over lunch, Saturday has nobody booked so its bar is all red, and Sunday is closed, so its card is dashed and its bar is hatched, the same hatch as a shift nobody booked. Booked hours are ink and missing ones red; a day that is fully covered turns its whole bar green, and a day with nobody booked is all red. The cards and the Cover row follow the same rule. Below them, the chosen day runs full width against an hour scale with faint hour lines, one bar per person, their week total at the end of the row, and one “now” line through every bar. Each day is drawn to scale. A day’s track is the shop’s open hours that day, printed under its name, and a shift fills the part it covers, so a short Friday looks short and 4pm to close looks empty. Past shifts are grey, future ones ink, and whoever is on now is green, with a notch at the current minute, as on the counter cards. Mike’s unbooked shift is hatched from 12:10 to close. Sunday is closed, so its column is narrow rather than a seventh of the width spent on nothing. Each person’s week total sits under their name, and Ana’s row keeps its empty tracks like anyone’s day off, with Set hours as a link in her line.

The last row is cover: everyone’s booked hours together, with the gaps red and labelled. Narrower, the same component sheds rather than shrinks: the day buttons lose their bars and become short names, and the avatars, week totals and hour lines drop, leaving the bars and the now line.

The team is the people and what each may do. One row per person, with four permissions in fixed columns: the tablet, refunds, payouts and staff. A check is allowed, a dash is not, and a figure means up to that figure, so Luis’s $500 is the refund limit on the right, applied to a person. Ana’s tablet column says No PIN, because she sets one on her first shift.

Below that, the team fills one column and the roles sit above the refund limit in the other — one slab, not two stacked with a seam between them. Who is on the tablet, what each role can do, and the one setting that matters, all on the screen at once. Charge counts per person are the useful by-product of the shift PIN — they show who is actually offering it, which is how an owner finds out why one shop converts and another does not.

Hours

One modal, reached from the person: the Hours row on their sheet, or Ana’s slot while she has none. There is no schedule on the page. An owner sets hours now and then, and the page is for who is on right now.

Days, then hours, then Save. Most people at a small counter work the same hours on the days they work, so that is one pair of times, not seven. A tap on either time opens half hours inside the shop’s opening hours.

Every week or this week only is the only other choice, and it sits at the top because it decides what Save does. A usual-hours change starts next week, so it never rewrites the week someone is in. A this-week change is a one-off, and it says when the usual hours come back. The total above Save is the thing an owner checks first, so it is two figures, days and hours, each with its icon and what it counts. What Save will do sits in one line under them, in its own box, so it reads as a note about the button rather than more numbers. Home reads the booked end and length from here, and those give “Until 4:00pm” and the hour blocks. The start is always the PIN. The break allowance is one shop-wide setting rather than a per-person field.

Different hours on another day

The link goes one level deeper in the same sheet, with a back arrow, rather than opening a second modal on top of the first. It asks two things, which day and its hours, and nothing else. Picking a day she already works gives it its own hours. Picking a day she does not work adds it, so a Saturday morning is the same gesture as a short Friday.

Back in her hours, the day becomes a ruled row under the shared pair, with its own times and a cross to put it back to the shared hours. The shared pair relabels itself to the days it still covers, here Mon–Thu, so the two never disagree. The total above Save follows.

Two rules hold it together. A PIN can never raise its own limit, or a manager lifts their ceiling and then uses it. And every refund needs a PIN either way — this setting only decides whose.

Open

  • Where the $1,500.00 ceiling comes from. It is stated as the highest an owner can set, but nothing here says whether it is fixed, tied to the shop’s volume, or something support can lift.
  • A manager adding staff. The role description says a manager can add and remove counter staff, so Add someone should appear on a manager’s shift too — there is no manager-shift screen in this file yet.
  • Two people on at once. Hours can overlap, but the tablet holds one PIN at a time. The proposal: a shift runs from someone’s first PIN of the day until they end it, and Change shift swaps who is charging without stopping anyone’s clock.
  • A shift nobody booked. Starting one must always work. Home’s clock then shows time on and nothing else: no “left”, no hour blocks. That variant is not drawn yet.
  • A rota, not a timesheet. Starts, ends and breaks against a PIN look like time records, which carry real obligations in California. Either this stays clearly about who is on the counter, or it becomes a timesheet deliberately and with advice.
  • Removing someone mid-refund. If Jen has a refund waiting on Mike and is removed, the request should survive with her name on it. Nothing decides that yet.
