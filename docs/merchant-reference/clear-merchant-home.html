Home.

The counter tablet’s first screen, converted to the brand guide with the member app’s anatomy. Three states, two roles, three widths — and the sheets that open from it.

The rules this page keeps

  • Tablet first, and nothing is removed at any width. Above about 900px the tablet sits in a stand with action on the left and context on the right; between 520 and 900 it is one column with the action first; below that it is the phone. Only the arrangement changes.
  • Waiting is the action component, and it has an empty state. Three customers at a glance, a button to the rest, and a clear statement when nobody is waiting — it never disappears, because its absence would read as a fault.
  • Temporary actions are one panel of statements, each with a line of detail and a button, under the action component — the same component the Staff page uses for somebody waiting to start. Setup on day one, the tip when activity is thin, gone when done.
  • Guidance retires itself. Home is the only screen that has to hold a full tablet at every stage, so the space activity will fill is not left empty in the meantime — setup and training stand in for it, and each panel goes when the thing it was standing in for arrives.
  • The member app’s anatomy, unchanged. Hero on paper, a temporary slot that is absent when empty, a slab of cells on one seam. A waiting charge is exactly what that slot was built for.

Running · owner · tablet

Waiting is the action component, under the figure, with an empty state so it never vanishes on a quiet afternoon.

The temporary actions sit under it, in the shape the Staff page already uses: a plain panel, one row per thing, each a statement with a line under it and its own button, no header and no footer. Setup lives there on day one, the tip lives there when activity is thin, and a row goes when it is done.

The state a shop lives in, in the member Home’s three blocks: the figure, the temporary slot, the slab. Today counts confirmed charges only, with the waiting one stated beside it — a figure that includes money a customer has not agreed to is one an owner will stop trusting. The waiting charge takes the slot, bordered underway, with its delivery receipts: text, email, and the app opening, the last in cobalt because it is the one thing moving. That is what no card terminal can show a writer.

The slab below is what was confirmed on the left and where it goes on the right. The payout carries a bar, because it is one ratio: how much of it is available to withdraw today against how much is still settling — which is the question an owner has, and the reason the cell leads to Payouts, where withdrawing happens. Beneath it, who raised what: per-person counts are the by-product of the shift PIN, and they are how an owner learns who actually offers it.

Running · counter shift · tablet

The same screen on Jen’s shift, with the shop’s money taken out. A counter role never sees the payout, the rate or the monthly totals, so the payout cell becomes Your shift — and that is a time clock rather than a summary. Who is on, how long they have been on, what the shift has produced, and the two controls that manage it: start a break, or end it. The clock is a pair: how long Jen has been on, and how long is left, each with its end of the shift beneath it. The bar counts hours, one block per booked hour, like the split-pay segments, so four and a bit filled reads as four and a bit hours without a label. What the shift produced, and the drawer it is working from, sit in the same ruled rows as Confirmed today, so the two cells share one rhythm across the seam instead of a metrics grid fighting a list. The one row that is a job carries the dot and an underway figure — Nina’s charge — because a number in a column of numbers does not look like something to do. On a break the clock greys and stops and the chip says how long (shown below) — a writer who forgets to come back should be able to see it from across the counter. Payouts and Overview stay in the nav with a padlock rather than disappearing — a writer learns where they are, and an owner knows where to sign in.

The drawer

Cash is the one kind of money the app cannot count for itself, so the drawer gets three moments and no more. The first person on counts it in; whoever is on sees it in their shift cell, what it started with and what has gone in; and at close it is counted out twice, by two people, blind. Counting is note by note, two to a row with each note’s total under it, because that is how a drawer is actually counted, with the total there to type for anyone who counted by hand. Expected, counted and the difference sit in one ruled strip, and a difference asks for one line about what happened rather than a form.

Fixed from the old reference

  • The counter shift was shown the payout. The old Home was Jen’s shift — her initials in the corner — with a $4,218.91 payout on the right, which the staff rules say a counter role never sees. The profile sheet showed her the rate and the cash account too. Both are role-aware now.
  • Today counted a charge nobody had agreed to. $1,840.00 included the $940.00 still waiting. Today is confirmed charges only, with the waiting amount beside it.
  • The early tip contradicted its own list. It said Jen raised both of today’s charges while the row beneath showed Luis raising one. Both are Jen’s now, which is the only version where the tip is true.
  • The staff tip was on a shared screen. It is owner-only and says so.
  • The waiting charge was a box inside a column. It carried its own border inside the slab’s, so the two doubled up, and it stretched the left column until the payout cell beside it was mostly empty. It is its own block now, between the figure and the slab, which is where the member Home already keeps its temporary slot.
  • The rate disagreed with itself. The new-charge screen charged 2.5%, and so does the refund example, while the profile sheet said 2%. Everything here is 2.5%, which is the standard rate.
  • Square avatars. The old reference used squircles to separate accounts from people. The guide squares everything that is drawn, so the distinction is carried by the chip beside a name instead.

Open

  • A close with one person on. A food truck at 10pm has nobody to check the count. A setting could allow one count when only one person is on, with the sign-off coming from the owner the next morning.
  • What the counter nav does with owner items. Dimmed with a padlock and leading to Owner sign in is the proposal here. The alternative — removing them on a counter shift — keeps the nav honest about what a writer can do, but makes it a different shape depending on who is holding the tablet.
  • When the tip retires. Here it goes once every writer on the counter has raised a charge. It could equally persist as a weekly line in Overview, where an owner is already looking at who does what.
